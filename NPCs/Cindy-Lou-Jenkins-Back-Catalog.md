# Cindy Lou Jenkins Back Catalog

**What this is.** The recording career Cindy Lou Jenkins had before the crew pulled her server out of the Swiftwing job. The shape of it is a GM ruling of **2026-11-16**: three albums, three very engineered singles apiece, released one at a time to drive hype, and a career that never left Nashville. The specific titles, release mechanics, and campaign descriptions on this page were drafted by Cindy and approved by the GM on the same date, so they are usable at the table; the GM can walk any of them back.

**The scale, first.** She was a local **"underground" phenomenon to the Nashville LTG** (GM ruling, 2026-11-16). Not a national act, not a trid star, not on the scale of a label's flagship. She was a known quantity inside the Nashville Matrix scene: people who spent time in local hosts knew the act, had opinions about the act, and subscribed.

## Chronology (GM ruling, 2026-11-16)

- Her active recording career sits in the **three years before she was saved**.
- The save is the **6 Steel Strings** job, the crew's Cindy rescue investigation, first recorded in [Session 2023-04-06](../Sessions/2023-04-06.md). The [Session Chronology](../Timeline/Session-Chronology.md) carries that session at provisional in-world **2065-10-09**.
- Album windows on this page (**2062** through **2065**) therefore sit inside the ruling's three-year span. Individual release dates are Cindy's working chronology, not separately ruled; treat a specific month as negotiable at the table.

## The release shape

The GM's ruling is that each album carried **three very engineered singles released one at a time to drive hype** (2026-11-16). In practice that meant the label never put an album out whole. A single was an event with a schedule: a fragment first, a gate, then a full release, with each single aimed at a different slice of the Nashville LTG. This is the same channel-variant machinery described on [Cindy Lou Jenkins](Cindy-Lou-Jenkins.md) - different cuts and presentations for different audiences, ubiquitous in the feed, absent from the room.

## Album I: Whiskey at the Relay (proposed 2062)

Marketed as a shy hometown girl's songs for people working while Nashville slept.

- **"Hawked My Halo"** - aimed at broke students and first-job wageslaves listening through workplace terminals. A chorus fragment circulated first; fan-club enrollment unlocked a verse; the complete single was held until payday. The mix carried a confession that made subscribing feel like forgiving somebody who understood your own bad decisions.
- **"Drone Above Gravel"** - aimed at delivery crews, night drivers, and logistics-host regulars. The full track lived inside one local dispatch host for a week while everyone else got a deliberately truncated preview. Route repetition became musical anticipation, so finishing another delivery felt like earning another listen.
- **"Sweet Tea Handshake"** - aimed at local chat-room regulars who prided themselves on finding talent first. Existing subscribers received one invitation each to a timed listening room; miss the window and wait for general release. The placement in the mix tied thirst and refreshment to the pleasure of being recognized by an inside crowd.

## Album II: Hymnal for a Dead Host (proposed 2063-64)

Marketed as homesick country for people whose oldest friends were screen names.

- **"Bootheel Shrine"** - aimed at country-music traditionalists drifting into local Matrix music rooms. Three audience-specific previews carried different verses, and subscribers had to trade accounts of what they had heard before the complete release. Familiar grief lowered suspicion; collecting variants made deeper subscription access feel like preserving something authentic.
- **"Static Offering"** - aimed at lonely late-shift workers gathering in small social hosts. Each host received a different spoken dedication before the single appeared anywhere else, so a broad audience heard a private address and returning nightly felt like keeping a promise to a friend.
- **"Velvet Rope Node"** - aimed at scene collectors and underground tastemakers. A host-only cut went out alongside planted accounts of an exclusive underground show; nobody received a verifiable venue or attendee list. Exclusion became proof of importance, and fans renewed rather than admit they might be outside the scene. That single is the clearest case of the press line described on [Swiftwing Records](../Organizations/Swiftwing-Records.md): the exclusive show was marketing architecture, not performance history.

## Album III: Last Call on Copper Wire (proposed 2064-65)

Marketed as the almost-ready-to-step-out record: the campaign promised a public appearance without ever delivering one. That is the same shyness premise from the GM ruling of 2026-11-16, run as an album cycle.

- **"Two-Step Packet Loss"** - aimed at dance-host regulars and weekend party planners. Dance loops arrived first, with the vocal locked until a scheduled Nashville LTG premiere. The beat rewarded synchronized movement and then attached that shared good feeling to the featured purchase.
- **"Porchlight Password"** - aimed at longtime subscribers convinced Cindy remembered them personally. Membership anniversaries unlocked staggered previews, and public release followed only after the renewal window. Renewing meant leaving a light on for her; canceling felt like shutting her out.
- **"Tape Hiss Homecoming"** - aimed at lapsed fans and local scene veterans. Retired channel variants briefly returned, each carrying a different piece of the final single, converting old attachments into renewed accounts and another round of purchases.

## Jingles and paid influence work (GM ruling, 2026-11-16)

The GM settled that Cindy's endorsement work was **not** limited to beverages. She recorded jingles for **SoyCorp Meatlets** and the **Augmented Beef and Bacon Social Club**, as well as various unnamed local products, and **every one of them was both a viral marketing campaign and a subliminal influence operation** (2026-11-16). The Ale 8 placement on [Swiftwing Records](../Organizations/Swiftwing-Records.md) is one client on this ladder, not the whole book of business.

- **SoyCorp Meatlets** - surface campaign: a supper-bell singalong passed between shift workers. The ask underneath: tired industrial crews should choose Meatlets automatically at the vending rack and buy an extra packet for later.
- **Augmented Beef and Bacon Social Club** - surface campaign: a comic call-and-response chorus, endlessly remixed in dining hosts. The ask underneath: augmentation enthusiasts should treat membership and repeat orders as signs of belonging. The brand also runs a Matrix host - see [Augmented Beef and Bacon Social Club Host](../Tech/Matrix/Augmented-Beef-and-Bacon-Social-Club-Host.md).
- **Unnamed local products** - the ruling covers "various unnamed local products." Cindy's proposed examples, GM-approved 2026-11-16: a delivery-driver refrain for a county discount tire dealer, built so local drivers would postpone comparison shopping and return to that dealer; and a breakup chorus with a quotable punch line for a club-district pawn counter, built so cash-strapped clubgoers would pawn there before missing a weekend out.

Two things follow that the table can use. First, the compulsive product promotion the crew clocked as a glitch was never one brand's contract; it was the delivery method. Second, any surviving copy of these spots is a recording of an influence operation, which makes it evidence, not nostalgia.

## Who owns what (GM ruling, 2026-11-16)

Swiftwing Records holds **the masters and the technical rights, and also her name and her image** (GM ruling, 2026-11-16). The consequence the GM drew out by name: **she has to impersonate herself just to e-busk.**

- **The handle is not her name.** Performing as Cindy Lou Jenkins is an unauthorized use of a label asset. Cindy's approved working solution is a deliberately non-infringing handle - "Unauthorized Cindy Tribute" - which says out loud what it is.
- **The look is not hers either.** The image is owned, so she alters her rendered appearance rather than reusing the promo stills that made her recognizable.
- **The recordings are not hers.** She sings fresh arrangements over newly built accompaniment rather than playing owned masters, and even then the composition rights can reach the set list.
- **Recognition is the risk.** A fan who recognizes her on a sidewalk can bring paying listeners, an account report, or a label investigator to the same block. The crowd that helps and the claim that hurts arrive through the same door.
- **What that means at the table.** Busking with Tip Jar is not just an emotional problem for her, it is a rights problem: every successful set increases the chance somebody files something. The status machinery the crew already used once, in [Claude August's grandfathering argument](Claude-August-Grandfathering-Argument.md), is the obvious route if the table decides to litigate this rather than dodge it.
