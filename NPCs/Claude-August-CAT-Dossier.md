## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Cross Applied Technologies has a leak, and the leak has a corner office.

Over the past eleven quarters, CAT's Southeast Division has lost a pattern of technology that no reasonable investigator can attribute to incompetence, industrial espionage by the usual suspects, or bad luck. The losses are not random. They are curated. Somebody with taste has been taking exactly the components that make our aerospace guidance stack work, and leaving the expensive parts nobody would want.

This office's assessment: **The Princeps Group, Ltd., of Huntsville, California Aurora Sector, is the receiving end.** More precisely, its chief executive, Claude August, is.

That assessment carries a professional cost, and I will be honest about it in a written file because I would rather be honest in a file than in a disciplinary hearing. The conventional reading of "a dragon personally stole eleven guidance assemblies out of a sealed CAT facility" is that we are overstating the case. This office's position is that we are understating it. A corporate thief who takes only what he understands, takes nothing twice, and takes nothing he cannot immediately justify on a manifest is not an amateur getting lucky. He is a collector.

We are not collecting evidence to arrest Claude August. We do not have standing, the CAS courts would not hear us, and arresting a great dragon is not an outcome available to a Nashville mid-cap. We are collecting evidence to do three cheaper things:

1. **Price him correctly.** Every Princeps counteroffer we have accepted in three years has been priced off our own cost basis rather than his actual cost basis. He has had our cost basis since roughly the beginning.
2. **Deny him the quiet parts.** The thefts we can prove are the thefts he no longer needs. Assume the ones we cannot prove are the ones that matter.
3. **Understand his premises.** Both of them. Section 4 and Section 5 of this file describe the two addresses associated with the subject in exhaustive physical, electronic, and astral detail, because a man who is going to keep taking things has to keep them somewhere, and somewhere has rules.

## 2. SUBJECT: WHAT WE KNOW HE IS

**Claude August.** Male human presentation, apparent age mid-forties, height and build consistent with the presentation he has chosen. Chief Executive Officer and controlling interest of The Princeps Group, Ltd., Huntsville, CAS — precision munitions guidance, hardened avionics, auric-insulated comms housings, and a growing line of "heritage" computing that nobody outside the CAS seems to want but which he keeps expanding.

Princeps does not hide what its principal is. This is not a cover story; it is a very effective one, because it makes anyone who suggests wrongdoing sound like a competitor throwing mud at a local hero.

**Public persona.** Generous employer, museum donor, patron of the Huntsville aviation heritage effort, a man who shows up at ribbon cuttings personally instead of sending a vice president. In our market research he polls better than CAT in the Huntsville metro by a margin that should embarrass our regional team and does.

August does not create martyrs. He understands that a dead guard is an insurance investigation and a live guard is a witness report that says "nothing happened."

**He has never once appeared where the evidence is.** Our best sensor footage of any Princeps-linked acquisition shows a delivery vehicle, a contractor badge, or a man in a good suit three blocks away looking interested in a storefront. The subject has an unbroken public record during every event window we can date. This office considers that an alibi problem of his own construction, and therefore a map of how he believes we can see him.

## 3. THE MOUNTAIN: MONTE SANO

Every acquisition we can date puts a Princeps vehicle somewhere on the north face of Monte Sano Mountain inside a ninety-minute window. Not in Huntsville proper. Not at the plant. The mountain.

For a company that makes guided munitions, our people's ignorance of local terrain is embarrassing, so here it is in the plainest terms our procurement officers can manage.

### 3.1 What the mountain actually is

Monte Sano is the southernmost finger of the Cumberland Plateau, standing alone above the Tennessee Valley floor — a flat-topped slab of conglomerate and sandstone left behind when everything around it eroded away. The important thing about that sentence is the order of the rocks.

The **cap** is tough: Mississippian-age Pottsville sandstone and conglomerate, abrasion-resistant, slow to weather, and responsible for the cliff line that rings the mountain like a wall you cannot see from the valley. Under the cap sit the limestones — Monteagle and Bangor limestones, thick, competent beds that dissolve. Where limestone dissolves, it makes voids. Where it makes voids and the cap above it falls in, it makes **sinkholes and vertical shafts**. Where groundwater keeps working the shaft, it makes caves.

The mountain has all three. Our own survey (Contract: Ridge & Vale Geotechnical, "hydrology reconnaissance, budget-limited," unclassified) found the well-known collapse features on the eastern slope, including the famous vertical shaft that the county puts on its hiking maps and its tourism brochures — a sinkhole that drops roughly sixty metres before letting you walk off sideways into limestone passage. It also found two dry quarry openings on the southern shoulder where somebody took dimension stone out of the hill before the mountain became a park, and it found the drainage that disappears in one sink and reappears in a spring a mile downstream. That drainage is the important one. A stream that vanishes underground travels through a channel it has had a few million years to enlarge.

Corporate Security's conclusion: **there is a working cave system beneath the caprock of Monte Sano, and it is accessible from more than one point.** Ridge & Vale flagged the same conclusion in their cover letter and then asked to be taken off the project. We paid them and took them off it.

### 3.2 Why a collector would want it

Read the surface history and the mountain is a very old habit of hiding things on this specific hill.

- The Cherokee used the plateau top; the early Nashville Road came up the slope and the mountain was renamed, in the vanity of the 1830s, for the fact that people who lived on it did not die of fever the way people in the bottoms did. *"Healthy mountain."* That is a place people go when they want to be left alone.
- The hill hosted a war camp in 1861–62, first for the secessionists and then for the Federals, with rifle pits on the summit ridge. Two armies camped on that plateau and neither of them found what was under it, because what is under it does not announce itself to men who are worried about other men.
- The television transmitter farm on the crest burned in 1982, and the county never rebuilt the full facility. A burned transmitter site on a public mountain means dead steel, a concrete pad, buried conduit runs, and an abandoned utility easement on the public record. To a man with a survey, that is a tunnel portal with paperwork already on it.
- The park around it is beloved, funded at the minimum, and patrolled by volunteers with radios. There are hiking trails, an astronomy society that drives up on clear nights, and one old lodge road that appears on four different maps with four different alignments.

A cave under that is not hidden. It is *unwatched*, which is different and much better. Nobody is looking at the mountain because nobody believes there is anything on the mountain to look at.

### 3.3 Assessed layout

We have not entered. Everything below is inference from surface observation, thermal work, the geotech report, and the assumption that the subject has taste.

**Primary access, assessed: the utility easement.** Abandoned transmitter pad, north-central slope. Our thermal passes over eleven months show a warm anomaly at the pad's downhill corner in every cold month, and none of the others. Concrete pad plus buried conduit plus a warm anomaly means a void below with air being moved through it. Somebody is ventilating a room.

**Secondary access, assessed: a shaft on the eastern slope.** Not the one on the tourism map. A neighbour of it, forty to sixty metres off, without a name and with a rusted grate we did not lift. Ridge & Vale walked past it and wrote "collapse feature, no continuity," which is either true or the most expensive sentence in this file.

**Tertiary access, assessed: the southern quarry openings.** Two dry portals, screen-walled and signed for bat habitat. Bat gates are a genuinely clever cover because they are legally protected, physically obvious, and every honest person in Huntsville has already been told not to bother with them.

**Interior, assessed.** Beyond the caprock the beds go to limestone, and limestone caves are walkable, humid, and quiet. Our inference is a working level cut into an existing passage — the easy part of any cave is the part that already exists — with three functional zones, because a thief needs three functional zones:

1. **Intake and scrub.** Where a thing comes in and stops being traceable. Degaussing, degassing, de-Matrixing. A cave stream is close to hand and cold; a cave stream is also an infinite sink for anything you want to have stopped existing.
2. **The collection itself.** Not racks. He is not a chop shop. Our read is a lined dry chamber above the water table, temperature-stable without mechanical effort, with the eleven items arranged the way a man arranges things he intends to look at again.
3. **The long room.** Where he keeps the parts he is not ready to use. Princeps does not need a second machine shop; it needs somewhere to put a machine shop that will never appear on a depreciation schedule.

**Occupancy.** We have never observed personnel at any of the assessed points. That is not evidence of an empty cave; it is evidence that the subject does not have staff in the cave. A dragon does not need a night watchman to be safe. He needs the building.

## 4. THE HOUSE: BURITT ON THE MOUNTAIN

The second address is no secret, which is precisely why it took us four months to stop treating it as a decoy.

**3101 Burritt Drive SE, Huntsville, CAS.** The Burritt Mansion, listed on the National Register of Historic Places, sitting on Round Top — the small plateau that stands off the main mass of Monte Sano Mountain like a shoulder standing in front of an arm. Read that again in the light of Section 3. **His vault and his front door are on the same mountain.** The cave and the conservatory are eleven minutes apart by car and about forty minutes apart on foot, and everything the subject owns that matters is on one landmass with one weather pattern and one set of access roads.

### 4.1 How he got the house

The house is a civic trust, and he did not steal it; he married it. Dr. William Henry Burritt (1869–1955), Huntsville physician, holder of twenty-two patents out of the St. Louis rubber trade, returned home in 1934 a widower and built himself an eccentric retirement estate of 167 acres on Round Top — part goat farm, part museum of a life. He designed the house himself, with Edgar Love's technical assistance on the record only as "possible." On 6 June 1936, the day he moved in, the house burned down. An electrical fire, fed by his beloved straw insulation and the metal panels protecting it. He rebuilt it, completed 1938, lived in it until he died, and left the whole estate to the City of Huntsville to be a museum, having no heirs.

That is a genuinely American story and the subject has used it flawlessly. When Princeps Group announced the "Heritage Curatorship" in 2069 — a ninety-nine-year lease-back of the mansion and its immediate grounds to a private curatorial foundation, with the museum operation, the endowment, and the twelve relocated nineteenth-century buildings all remaining with the city — half the town called it philanthropy and the other half called it a land grab by the usual tech money. Both halves were correct. He now resides in the mansion of record for Madison County's own heritage, as **curator-in-residence**, in the human identity of Claude August, and every photograph of him in the local paper is a photograph of him in front of a public trust.

You cannot subpoena a town's childhood home. Try it in a CAS court and see how the jury looks at you.

### 4.2 The building

The mansion is a two-storey octagonal central block with four one-storey wings arranged in the shape of an **X**, an oddity that Dr. Burritt chose and no fire marshal since has forgiven. Exterior walls are clad in grey asbestos-cement shingles stamped to imitate stone, so the house photographs as a stone house and tests, our people report, as something more stubborn than it looks. All roofs are flat and hidden behind a stepped parapet with concrete caps — every surface that an intruder would walk on is a parapet with no visible edge to judge. The one exception is the two-storey pedimented entry portico on two fluted Doric columns, with a small balcony above the door; the pediment panel reads **1935**, the date of the house that burned, which the subject has pointed out to at least two journalists is "the honest date, the one the first builder would have wanted kept."

The front door is Federal style, sidelights and fanlight, opening onto a terrace that runs the length of the house. First-floor windows are cased, eight panes to the side with an eight-pane transom panel above — casements, meaning **they open**, meaning the oldest vector in the book is still on the table, which is why the transoms are the interesting part (see 4.3). Chimneys stand at the ends of the north, south, and west wings, with two interior stacks in the north wing and one in the centre of the main block: **five flues, four of which are unused, and a dragon does not need a chimney, but a man who wants people to believe he is a man does.**

Internally the plan is X-shaped and therefore radial: the entry hall with its spiral staircase is the centre of the wheel and every wing is a spoke with no way to cross between spokes without passing the hub. The west wing is a large parlor; the south wing is the master suite — study and bedroom; the dining room sits northeast in the main block, with the north wing carrying the kitchen and a bedroom and the east wing a sitting room and a bedroom. Off the rear, on the east end, is the conservatory. Upstairs, front of the house, is an **observatory room** with a view of the city below, with a bedroom and small kitchen behind it. Our people keep describing the observatory as a quirk of the original doctor's eccentricity. This office notes that a man who owns the sky above a missile-capital's test range might value a room whose only described purpose is *looking at things far away without being seen doing it*, and that the spiral staircase at the hub means **the centre of this house can be defended by one occupant against four spokes.** Do not send four teams down four corridors. It is a kill-box and the blueprints are on the internet.

The grounds are the harder half. The estate holds twelve relocated nineteenth-century structures — the Balch House of 1887 from near Harvest, the Chandler dogtrot from Lester in Limestone County, an 1884 church out of downtown Madison, the 1845 Meals cabin, the Smith-Williams house of 1868 (two 1840s structures joined, from McKay Hollow at the foot of Monte Sano), an 1890s barn from near Minor Hill, Tennessee, and the Joel Eddins house of 1810 from near Ardmore, moved up in 2007 — plus modern work: a welcome centre, an administration building, a four-room Rosenwald school replica, and an events centre built to look like the demolished Hotel Monte Sano. That is thirteen buildings, a gift shop, a seasonal volunteer population, public event traffic, and the Madison County heritage calendar, all of it sitting between us and the octagon.

### 4.3 Assessed security — physical and electronic

Assessed against SR3 doctrine, printed rules where they apply, with our confidence marked honestly.

**Perimeter (grounds).** The old estate boundary is fenced along its service side. Manmade perimeter barriers of that class rate per the barrier materials table (SR3, p. 236, cross-ref. Barriers p. 124); the practical consequences are the printed ones — **firearms generally cannot damage a fence because rounds pass through the latticework**, and getting over it is an Athletics test, meaning it is a *time* barrier, not a stop barrier. The time is what he is buying. Wire is fitted to the sections flanking the service gate: our observers report **concertina** on the visible line, and the visible line is always the wrong one. Assume monowire on the section nobody photographs. Trip wires, if fitted, have the same Visibility as monowire (SR3, p. 236) — you do not see them, you meet them — and most do no damage when tripped beyond the alarm they raise.

**Access control.** The museum side runs ordinary civic kit. The private side does not. Our assessment is a layered maglock installation (SR3, pp. 235–236):

- **Staff/service, keypad, assessed Rating 6.** Keypad systems run Rating 0 to 10, and short of knowing the code the only route is rewiring, which is two steps: defeat the casing with an Electronics (B/R) test against a target number equal to the casing's Barrier Rating — usually equal to the system rating — and then survive the internal circuitry, which announces itself when cut. Rating 6 on a house in Huntsville is not heritage wiring.
- **Private wing, cardreader plus print scanner, assessed Rating 8.** Print scanners are defeated the way keypads are, **at +4 to the target number** (SR3, p. 236), which tells any competent infiltrator to stop trying and start copying credentials instead. Expect forged credsticks and a stolen thumb to be the first wave, not the last.
- **The study, voice recognition, assessed Rating 7.** Voice systems are the one layer with an active component: the checkpoint demands a response from an approved voice inside a time limit and sounds the alarm if none comes, and its circuitry is not even in the room — only a microphone and a speaker are, so there is nothing physical there to tamper with. It is defeated only by *speaking* with an approved voice — a recording, a simulation, or the real thing — as an Opposed Test of system rating against the rating of the deception gear (SR3, p. 236), Rating 1 being a pocket recorder and Rating 6 a professional deck, voice-modulator cyberware also on the table. Ties produce nothing and the system asks again. We assess he personally selects people whose voices the house knows. **The room's lock is a friendship test.**

**Active measures (SR3, p. 236).** Security company personnel on the grounds line, with the overnight count deliberately low and public: three guards is a museum budget. Do not believe the count, believe the composition. Our read is two contract guards plus at least one **security rigger** running the camera coverage as a patrol-drone complement, plus **guard animals**. The animals are the tell. Two of the dogs on the grounds are not dogs — see 4.4 — and a museum in Huntsville does not need a dog that will not eat near the conservatory.

**Sensor coverage.** The camera plan leaves two deliberate gaps on the terrace line. Nobody leaves deliberate gaps by accident, and both of them face the wing that holds his study. Whatever the cameras are not allowed to see is the room we need.

### 4.4 Assessed security — astral

This section is contracted work. It was performed by a freelance astromancer on CAT paper, paid by the hour, and she asked for a 30 percent hazard premium for the second pass and then did not return the call. What follows is her report plus my own reading of the gaps.

**What is missing is the first finding.** There is **no active astral security** (Magic in the Shadows, p. 88): no security-magician patrols, no bound elemental on a twenty-four-hour guard rotation, no nature spirit held to the grounds until sunrise or sunset. Active astral security is the best kind and the expensive kind, and a man with a great dragon's resources who has declined to buy it is telling us something. He is not telling us he is careless. He is telling us the passive layers are not for us.

**What is present:**

- **Wards** (Magic in the Shadows, p. 89, standard rules per SR3 p. 174). The private wing sits inside a ward of assessed **Force 6**, which is to say it is an astral barrier first and an alarm second: an astral intruder must win a Success Contest against the barrier, **ties going to the barrier**, and if the intruder wants to come back through later they must win another contest to do it. Every crossing is a fight. Her assessment is that the ward was not raised by the subject personally — the signature is institutional, consistent with a contracted hermetic circle doing an annual commission for a curatorial foundation.
- **Alarm wards** at the two wings he uses at night. These present as a faint shimmer rather than a barrier and can be spotted with an Astral Perception test modified by the ward's Force as a target number modifier; they notify their creator when an unauthorised astral entity crosses. They cost half the time of a standard ward to make, which is why there are more of them than a reasonable property needs.
- **A polarised ward on the conservatory.** This is the single most interesting object in the file. A polarised ward behaves like mirrored glass: opaque from one side, essentially invisible from the other, and those on the clear side see everything on the opaque side. Noticing the clear side requires an Astral Perception (8) test. It acts as an astral barrier **in both directions**. Our reading: **he has a room from which he can watch astral space over his own house, from a direction nobody can see him watching from, with a two-way wall between himself and what he is watching.** The conservatory is not a greenhouse and the man tending orchids in there at dusk is not tending orchids.
- **A masking ward over the study wing**, contracted to an astromancer with masking, making everything inside its bounds read as mundane on the astral. Our astromancer reported the study wing as "clean." That report is itself the detection: nothing in Huntsville is clean, and a report of clean in this zip code is a signature. **Treat every prior astral survey of the private wing as untrustworthy, including the ones we liked.**
- **Anchored spells** (Magic in the Shadows, p. 70) forming at least one reactive ward at the entry hall hub — an anchored, prepared magical response at the one point of the plan where everyone must pass.

**Counter-projection posture.** The subject is a dragon with Force 9 innate spells, astral capabilities, and an unbroken habit of never being where the evidence is. Assume he has accounted for projected astral intrusion the way a good bank accounts for tunnels: not by watching the vault, by watching the approach. Our recommendation against sending an astromancer alone into that house is unanimous and unarguable in this office.

## 5. THE PATTERN, AND WHAT WE DO ABOUT IT

Set the two addresses side by side and the case makes itself.

A man who keeps his collection in a cave under the Cumberland Plateau and his public face in a museum on the shoulder of the same mountain is not a man with two properties. He is a man with **one property and two doors**, connected by a private road that appears on no tour map, running through land his foundation controls, in a county that thinks it owns the second door and a park service that thinks it owns the mountain. Everything he cannot be seen carrying through the front door goes down the hill at night and comes back up it as an idea.

That is the theft thesis, stated plainly, and stated plainly it is also our problem. We cannot prove a single one of the eleven events. We can prove that he benefits from all eleven, that he has the means for all eleven, and that he is the only party in the market who ever knew which components mattered. In a court that would be enough to get a hearing. In the California Aurora Sector, against a great dragon with a philanthropy file, it is enough to get our division audited for wasting shareholder money on a grudge.

So we are not going to court. Corporate Security recommends four actions, none of which is "arrest him."

**1. Stop selling him the answer.** Every Princeps technical purchase should be repriced on the assumption that our cost basis is his cost basis. Where he has already taken an item, we should be selling him the successor at a premium he cannot decline precisely because we are the only source — and we should date the successor's public documentation to the day after the loss. If he buys the successor at our number, he has just told us the price of the thing he stole.

**2. Move the parts he has already catalogued.** The Decatur waveguides and the Scottsboro firmware are burned; he took them because he understood them. Anything he has taken once should be redesigned once, quietly, on a schedule that does not look like a reaction. He is a collector, and a collector's weakness is that he stops wanting what has already been completed. Let him keep finishing collections that stopped being ours two generations ago.

**3. Do not go into the cave.** This recommendation will be resisted, so I am putting it in writing before the meeting. The cave is on protected habitat land with legally posted bat gates, sits beneath a public park, and is probably a power site — see 5.1. Getting caught inside a sealed void on the north face of Monte Sano with our gear in our hands hands him a public-relations kill we will not survive, in a town that already likes him more than it likes us. The mountain is not the target. The mountain is the reason he thinks he is safe, and a man who thinks he is safe gets sloppy somewhere else.

**4. Watch the road between them.** Eleven minutes. That is all. One contractor with a traffic counter, a legitimate reason to be doing roadwork, and the patience of a saint. We do not need to know what is in the cave. We need to know what moves between the conservatory and the mountain on a Tuesday night, and we need to know it before he finds out that we know the two addresses are one address.

### 5.1 Addendum: the mana question

Freelance astromancer's preliminary finding, unconfirmed, and I want that printed in the file because the last thing this office needs is to be the ones who learned it from the trid.

Monte Sano is a mountain with a cave system, a vertical shaft, a vanished stream, and a plateau top that people have been using to hide things since the Cherokee. Magic in the Shadows notes that power sites and manalines cluster in exactly such places — the book's own list of typical power-site locations runs to hidden caves, grottoes, towers, **mountains**, wells, crossroads and cliffs (Magic in the Shadows, p. 84). The book is also explicit that most power sites are quite small and that human habitation tends to even out the mana level of an area, which is why a built-up valley floor reads clean while a hill the town has decided to leave as woods might not.

If the cave sits on a concentration, then the astral security our astromancer found lacking at the house may not be lacking at the vault — not because he bought anything, but because the ground itself raises the effective Force of whatever he puts down there and the drain he takes working in it is modified by the local background count (Magic in the Shadows, pp. 83–84). A dragon on a power site is not a magician on a power site. That is the difference between a secure room and a place where the laws of magic are somewhat more generous to the landlord.

**Recommendation stands. Do not go into the cave.** If we are going to be inside that mountain at all, we go in with a contract astromancer on retainer, an extraction plan that does not involve a sixty-metre shaft, and somebody other than me signing the authorisation.

— *Section Chief A. Ferreira, Corporate Security, Southeast Division*
